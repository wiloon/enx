Chapter One: The Harbour

The boat left the harbour before the sun had cleared the hills, and the old sailor stood at the rail watching the town grow small behind them. Clear from the long swells, the water turned from grey to a deep and restless green as they reached the open sea.

Nobody on board spoke much during the first hour. The wind was steady from the west, the sails were full, and every man seemed content to listen to the creak of the timbers and the hiss of the water along the hull as the land slipped away.

By noon the captain came up from his cabin with a chart rolled under his arm. He spread it across the hatch, weighed the corners down with a lantern and two stones, and traced their course with a finger that had lost its nail many years before.

The islands they were making for lay three days to the south, if the weather held. Beyond them, the chart showed only a scattering of reefs and a single line of handwriting that none of the crew could read, though several of them claimed to know what it said.

In the evening the cook carried a pot of stew up to the deck, and the men ate sitting on coils of rope while the light faded. Someone began a song about a girl in a northern port, and the others joined in at the chorus, loudly and badly, until the captain laughed and told them to stop.

The night watch fell to the youngest of them, a quiet boy who had never been further than the river mouth before this voyage. He kept his eyes on the horizon for hours, certain that every star rising out of the sea was the lamp of another ship.

When the morning came the wind had shifted, and with it the mood of the crew. Clouds were piling up in the east, heavy and dark at their edges, and the old sailor said quietly that they would be lucky to see the islands before the storm reached them.

They shortened sail before midday and lashed everything loose to the deck. The first rain came in hard, slanting sheets that stung the face and hands, and the sea began to rise in long ridges that lifted the boat and dropped it again into the troughs between.

For two days they ran before the weather, hardly sleeping, taking turns at the pump and the tiller. On the third morning the clouds broke apart, and through the gap the boy saw a low green shape on the water that did not move with the waves.

It was the first of the islands, closer than any of them had dared to hope. The captain took the tiller himself for the last mile, steering through a narrow passage in the reef while the crew stood silent at the rail and the surf thundered on either side.
