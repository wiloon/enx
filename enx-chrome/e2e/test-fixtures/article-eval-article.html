The Lighthouse Keeper

For thirty years the keeper climbed the spiral stairs every evening at dusk, carrying a can of oil and a cloth for the great lens. The island had no trees and no harbour, only a ring of black rocks where the supply boat could land when the sea was calm enough to allow it.

He kept a logbook in a careful hand, recording the wind, the weather and every ship that passed within sight of the tower. Some nights the fog was so thick that he rang the bell by hand until morning, his arms aching and his breath steaming in the cold air of the lamp room.

Visitors were rare. A painter came one summer and stayed for a month, sketching the cliffs and the seabirds that nested in them. She asked him whether he was ever lonely, and he answered that the sea was company enough for a man who had learned how to listen to it.

When the light was finally automated, the engineers found the logbooks stacked neatly in a cupboard, thirty volumes without a single missing day. They are kept now in the town museum, where children read the entries aloud and try to imagine the storms they describe.
